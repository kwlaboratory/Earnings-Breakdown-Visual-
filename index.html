01

Executive Overview

What it is, why it matters, and why now.

Key Insight

Elmet is not a space company or satellite play. It is a 97-year-old, Portland, Maine-based manufacturer that happens to be the only U.S.-owned, vertically integrated producer of tungsten and molybdenum — two metals that sit at the intersection of hypersonics, directed energy, semiconductors, and medical imaging. Since the April 2026 IPO, two quarters of public reporting have now confirmed accelerating growth: Q2 2026 revenue grew 35.2% YoY to $66.4M, and the AD&G backlog alone grew 100.5% YoY on programs including Precision Strike Missile, Standard Missile, Patriot, and CERN.

What It Is
Critical Materials + RF Systems
A manufacturer with two complementary divisions: CMC (refractory metals from powder to precision component) and EMP (high-power RF/microwave systems for radar, directed energy, and science). Both serve defense, aerospace, semiconductor, medical, and energy markets.
Why It Matters
Irreplaceable Domestic Monopoly
Management stated on the Q2 call that Elmet has sourced more than 95% of its tungsten and molybdenum from outside China for over a decade — a structural hedge most peers lack. Combined with U.S.-only ownership, this makes Elmet the last-resort domestic supplier for classified DoD programs that foreign-owned rivals cannot access.
Why Now
Defense Tailwind + Pricing Windfall
Global tungsten and molybdenum prices spiked sharply in Q1-Q2 2026, partly driven by export-control friction. Elmet's strategic sourcing agreements let it capture the upside on inventory already on hand — a real but potentially non-repeatable tailwind layered on top of genuine defense-driven demand growth.

Mental Model

The simplest accurate framing before adding complexity.

First Principles Frame

Think of Elmet as a picks-and-shovels play on extreme-environment manufacturing. Whether America is building hypersonic missiles, semiconductor fab equipment, MRI machines, or particle accelerators — all of them require metals that can withstand temperatures above 2,000°C and components that can handle megawatt-level electromagnetic fields. Elmet makes both the metals and the RF plumbing. It sits at an upstream chokepoint that is hard to replicate, domestically sovereign, and economically sticky.

The Simpler Analogy
The "Intel Inside" of Extreme Manufacturing
Just as a chip might be invisible but essential to a laptop, Elmet's tungsten sinker or waveguide coupler is invisible but irreplaceable in a radar system, missile, or medical scanner. The end customer pays billions for the system; Elmet captures a fraction of that but with high stickiness — once designed in, it is extremely difficult to swap out.
What Drives the Business
Three Compounding Forces
1. Defense Cycle: Rising U.S. and allied defense spending on munitions, hypersonics, missile defense, and radar programs increases demand for refractory metals and high-power RF components. On the Q2 call, management confirmed sizable missile-defense interceptor awards to primes have not yet flowed down into Elmet's backlog — implying more is coming.
2. Reshoring Mandate: U.S. government preference/requirement for domestic sourcing in classified and sensitive programs structurally excludes foreign-owned competitors.
3. Secular End-Market Growth: Semiconductor capital equipment expansion, fusion/fission energy investment (still early-stage per management), and medical imaging growth provide diversified, non-defense tailwinds.
What Makes This Complicated

Two divisions are moving in opposite margin directions. CMC gross margin is expanding rapidly on tungsten pricing capture and productivity gains. EMP margins declined in Q2 2026 due to factory execution issues and material costs on long-term contracts (CERN, Fermilab) rising faster than anticipated. Management candidly acknowledged this will "take a little while" to fix. The investment question: is CMC's margin strength durable and structural, or partly a one-time margin capture that fades as inventory turns?

Key Feedback Loop

Defense program design-in leads to a long qualification cycle (12-36 months), then multi-decade platform lock-in, then high-margin recurring revenue for consumables and replacement parts, then cash to reinvest in capacity and M&A, then more programs won. Management noted Elmet often "produces multiple years of production in one year" for multi-year AD&G programs — meaning current backlog conversion may understate steady-state run-rate demand once large prime-contractor flow-downs (e.g., missile defense interceptors) begin appearing.

05

Deep Dive

First-principles explanation of why this business is hard to replicate.

Why Tungsten and Molybdenum Are Irreplaceable

Tungsten has the highest melting point of any metal (3,422°C) and the highest tensile strength at elevated temperature. Molybdenum shares similar high-temperature stability with better machinability and lower density. Together they are the only practical materials for components that must survive extreme heat, radiation, or electromagnetic stress without deforming — missile nose cones, X-ray targets, semiconductor sputtering targets, and radiation shielding. There is no viable substitute chemistry at scale; this is a materials-science hard constraint, not a preference.

The Powder Metallurgy Barrier

Unlike most metals, tungsten and molybdenum cannot be cast conventionally — their melting points are too extreme for standard foundry equipment. Instead, producers use powder metallurgy: compress metal powder into a "green" part, then sinter it at temperatures near the melting point in controlled atmosphere furnaces. Getting consistent density, grain structure, and dimensional tolerance requires decades of proprietary process knowledge. Management describes this as a "difficult to replicate asset base... which took decades to develop" — this is the real moat, not patents or brand.

Hypersonics and Defense Demand Mechanics

Hypersonic weapons travel above Mach 5, generating leading-edge temperatures that exceed the melting point of most structural metals. Tungsten alloys are used for nose tips and control surfaces specifically because nothing else survives. As the U.S. and allies accelerate hypersonic and missile-defense programs (Precision Strike Missile, Standard Missile, Patriot per Q2 backlog commentary), demand for these specific alloys scales directly with program funding — a linkage investors can trace through DoD budget lines to Elmet's order book, albeit with a lag, since management confirmed large multi-year prime flow-downs "haven't hit backlog yet."

The EQ Resources Sourcing Hedge — Interpretation vs. Fact

Fact: Elmet increased its ownership stake in EQ Resources (a Western tungsten miner) in June 2026 as part of a strategic collaboration and long-term offtake agreement, and separately exercised an option to buy 20 million EQ Resources shares, reportedly raising its stake to 23.6 million shares. Interpretation: This looks like a deliberate vertical-integration-upstream move to lock in non-China tungsten supply ahead of further export-control escalation — consistent with management's decade-long strategy of >95% non-China sourcing. Open question: the economic terms of the offtake (pricing formula, volume commitments) have not been disclosed in earnings materials reviewed here, so the degree of margin protection this actually provides in a sustained price spike is not yet fully verifiable from public sources.

Measurement Challenge — Signal vs. Narrative

A meaningful share of Q2 2026 gross margin expansion (management estimated roughly half of the CMC growth) came from capturing a tungsten price spike on existing inventory rather than from structural productivity gains. This is real cash, but it is not necessarily a repeatable margin driver every quarter — commodity prices can fall as easily as they rise. Investors should separate the "productivity gain" narrative (durable) from the "pricing capture" narrative (cyclical) when assessing whether 25% gross margin is a new baseline or a peak.

Reading Through the GAAP Net Loss

Elmet swung to a GAAP net loss of $4.5M in Q2 2026 (vs. +$1.2M a year ago), driven almost entirely by $14.2M of stock-based compensation, of which $12.9M was a one-time charge tied to award vesting upon IPO completion. Operating expenses rose 251% YoY largely for this reason, plus new public-company compliance costs. Adjusted net income was actually up YoY to $5.2M ($0.18/share), beating consensus estimates of -$0.20 by a wide margin. This is a case where GAAP and adjusted numbers diverge sharply for a clearly identifiable, mostly non-recurring reason — investors should track whether SBC normalizes in coming quarters as the one-time vesting rolls off.

Balance Sheet Post-IPO
Meaningfully Delevered
IPO raised $125.4M net proceeds (April 2026). Company retired $17.5M of term debt and paid down $31.0M on its revolver. Cash at Q2 2026 end was $66.1M, up from $1.8M at year-end 2025. Total liquidity (cash + revolver capacity) stood at $110.7M — a substantial war chest for capacity expansion or bolt-on M&A.
Working Capital Watch Item
Inventory Building Fast
Inventories grew to $102.4M at Q2 2026 end, up from $75.0M in Q1 2026 and $67.1M a year ago — driven by rising raw material prices, sourcing dynamics, and volume growth. This is partly a strategic bet (locking in tungsten ahead of further price increases) and partly a natural consequence of growth, but it increases working-capital intensity and commodity price exposure on the balance sheet.
Valuation Context

At ~$500M market cap against $31.8M TTM adjusted EBITDA, ELMT trades around 15-16x TTM adjusted EBITDA — down from the ~20x+ multiple implied shortly after its April IPO, reflecting both share price movement and rapid EBITDA growth. Consensus sell-side price target is $20.50 (6 analysts: 4 buy, 1 hold, 1 sell), implying roughly 20% upside from current levels. Note that Morningstar's automated fair value model has shown volatile and inconsistent outputs for this newly public, thinly covered stock — treat single-source fair value estimates with caution given limited historical data and analyst coverage.

The Regulatory Moat, Explained Simply

Most global tungsten and molybdenum capacity sits with foreign-owned firms — largely European or Chinese-linked. For classified U.S. defense programs subject to ITAR and foreign-ownership restrictions, this eliminates most of the global supply base as eligible vendors. Management explicitly framed this on the Q2 call: "with most of our competitors owned by foreign entities, and as export restrictions cause more constraints, we remain one of the last U.S.-based facilities" that can serve these programs. This is a real, verifiable structural advantage — but it is also the reason Elmet trades at a premium multiple, since the market has already priced in scarcity value.

New Growth Vector — Orbital Compute / Satellite

Management disclosed on the Q2 call that Elmet has invested ~$3M in a new CMC production line for satellite component customers (not yet including SpaceX, per direct management comment) and is seeing rising EMP-side demand for drone-defense and space-based counter-drone systems. This is an emerging, unproven vertical — worth monitoring as a call option on the broader space/defense buildout, but not yet a material line item.

Variant Perception & Debates

What smart investors disagree about, and why.

  • +
    Structural, not cyclical, scarcity. U.S.-only ownership and vertical integration in tungsten/moly is nearly impossible to replicate quickly — the "decades to develop" asset base is a genuine time-and-capital moat, not a marketing claim.
  • +
    Backlog understates true demand. Management confirmed large prime-contractor missile-defense flow-downs haven't yet hit the order book — the current $131.5M backlog may be a floor, not a ceiling, for near-term demand.
  • +
    Margin mix-shift is real and quantifiable. Management gave a specific, falsifiable target (30% gross margin in 4-5 years) driven by AD&G mix shift — a trackable KPI, not vague guidance.
  • +
    Balance sheet is now clean. Post-IPO deleveraging and $110.7M of liquidity give management room to fund capacity expansion or accretive M&A without further dilution.
  • -
    Two quarters is not a track record. Elmet has been public since April 2026. The Q2 beat is real, but the sample size for judging management's ability to sustain 25%+ gross margins or hit the 30% target is extremely small.
  • -
    Margin quality is muddied by commodity timing. Roughly half of Q2's CMC margin gain came from capturing a tungsten price spike on inventory already on hand — a timing benefit, not a repeatable operating improvement. This makes headline gross margin numbers harder to extrapolate.
  • -
    EMP is underperforming and unresolved. Margin weakness at the EMP division was attributed to factory execution problems the company itself says will take time to fix — an open wound in a "vertically integrated excellence" narrative.
  • -
    Valuation already prices in the scarcity premium. At ~15-16x TTM adjusted EBITDA for a company with a two-quarter public history, thin sell-side coverage, and lumpy end markets, much of the "sole U.S. supplier" narrative may already be reflected in the price — leaving less room for multiple expansion even if fundamentals execute as promised.
The Unsettled Question

Is Elmet's Q2 2026 gross margin of 25.0% a new sustainable baseline reflecting real structural improvement, or a peak inflated by a one-time tungsten pricing capture that will partially revert as commodity markets normalize and inventory turns? Management's own commentary supports both readings simultaneously — genuine productivity gains occurred, but so did a real commodity timing benefit. Reasonable analysts can and do disagree on how to weight these two effects going into H2 2026 and FY2027.

Glossary

Key terms in plain English.

Tungsten / Molybdenum (W / Mo)
Refractory metals with extremely high melting points, used where components must survive extreme heat or radiation without deforming.
Powder Metallurgy
Manufacturing process that compresses and sinters metal powder into shape, used for metals too high-melting for conventional casting.
Sintering
Heating compressed metal powder just below its melting point to fuse particles into a solid, dense part.
AD&G
Aerospace, Defense & Government — Elmet's largest and fastest-growing end market segment.
CMC Division
Critical Materials Components — Elmet's tungsten/molybdenum manufacturing business, ~85% of revenue.
EMP Division
Engineered Microwave Products — Elmet's high-power RF/microwave systems business, ~15% of revenue.
Waveguide / Circulator
Precision RF components that direct or isolate microwave energy in radar and particle-accelerator systems.
ITAR
International Traffic in Arms Regulations — U.S. rules restricting defense-related exports and foreign involvement in sensitive programs.
Firm Order Backlog
Confirmed, unfulfilled customer orders — a leading indicator of near-term revenue, though conversion timing varies.
Design-In
When a component is specified into a customer's system design, creating high switching costs for the life of that platform.
Adjusted EBITDA
Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, amortization, stock-based comp, and one-time costs — management's preferred profitability metric.
Stock-Based Compensation (SBC)
Non-cash expense from equity awards to employees; spiked in Q2 2026 due to one-time IPO vesting.
EQ Resources
Western (non-China) tungsten mining company in which Elmet holds an equity stake and long-term offtake agreement.
Offtake Agreement
A contract to purchase a portion of a supplier's future production, typically used to secure raw material access and price stability.
Vertical Integration
Controlling multiple stages of production in-house — for Elmet, from raw powder through finished precision components.
TTM
Trailing Twelve Months — a rolling annual performance window management uses to smooth quarterly volatility.
